## How did I pick these five?

I picked these five because each one is a different answer to the same question: how does a practice get in touch with hundreds of patients it has not seen for two years, without the front desk doing it by hand? I judged them on four things.

- Can it start from the patient list you already have, rather than a new lead source?
- Does it use SMS and email, the channels patients actually read?
- Does a reply turn into a booking, or into another task for reception?
- Can a practice in the UK or EU buy it, and is it clear what it costs?

I left out US-only tools. Most of the reactivation results you see online come from US vendors, and I have not quoted any, because I could not find independent UK data on response rates. Treat any "35% of lapsed patients came back" claim as one vendor's best case.

## 4. A lapsed patient report plus phone calls

Most practice systems can show you who has not been in. Dentally's [lapsed patient guidance](https://www.dentally.com/en-gb/insights-hub/how-to-the-cold-patient-report) describes a Lapsed Patients report that lists every patient who has not visited since a date you choose, and suggests using today's date plus twice your longest recall interval. Staff then phone each patient to offer a check-up. The same exercise lets you archive deceased patients and remove outdated phone numbers and addresses.

I like this as a first step even if you buy something later, because the data clean-up is the part everyone skips. The weakness is the same as any manual process. It works the week someone has time and stalls the week the diary is full. Phone calls to patients who have not heard from you in two years also take longer than a text.

- Best for: practices with a quiet reception and a list that is small enough to ring through.
- Price: the Dentally page states none, so check what your own plan includes.
- Why it stands out: no new software, and it forces a clean list.
- Worth knowing: it is fully manual, and it does not send an SMS or email on its own.

Mailchimp is the do-it-yourself route for email. Its [pricing page](https://mailchimp.com/pricing/marketing/) shows a Free plan with a limit of 250 contacts and 500 sends a month, Essentials from 12.01 EUR per month for the first 12 months, Standard from 18.47 EUR per month for 12 months, and Premium from 323.31 EUR per month for 12 months. You would export the lapsed list from your practice system, upload it, write the emails and send them. It works, and it is cheap. What it will not do is read a reply, offer a slot or write a booking into your diary. And a practice with several thousand dormant patients will quickly outgrow the free tier.

- Best for: practices that want to try email only, with someone happy to build and send it.

- Why it stands out: low cost and plenty of templates.
- Worth knowing: no booking link to your diary, SMS is an add-on with no published price, and the introductory price runs for 12 months.

## Do you need consent to message lapsed patients?

You usually need either specific consent or a valid soft opt-in, and you need an opt-out in every message. The ICO's [guidance on email and text marketing](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/guide-to-pecr/electronic-and-telephone-marketing/electronic-mail-marketing/) says you can market to your own existing customers about a similar service without new consent if you gave them a clear chance to opt out when you collected their details and in every message. It does not cover bought-in lists.

In practice that gives you a short checklist before any send:

- Check what patients agreed to when they registered, and whether an opt-out was offered.
- Remove anyone who has told you to stop, and anyone you know has left the area.
- Put an opt-out in every message and act on it fast.
- Keep the first message about their care (a check-up is due), not a discount blast.

The ICO page also notes its guidance is under review because of the Data (Use and Access) Act, so check the current version, and ask your data protection lead before you send. This is not legal advice.

## A worked example: sending to a list of 1,200 lapsed patients

This is an illustration, not a client result. Say your system shows 1,200 patients not seen in over two years. You clean the list first and remove the deceased, the moved and the opted out, which might leave you around 900 (a made-up figure, yours will differ). You send a first text to 100 of them, to see how replies and complaints look. If that goes well, you send the rest in batches over the following weeks so the phone lines and the diary can cope, with a second message to anyone who has not replied.

The batching is the part people get wrong. A good campaign that lands 60 replies on a Monday morning can swamp reception and lose half of them. That is why I care more about whether a reply books itself than about which tool writes the nicest text.
