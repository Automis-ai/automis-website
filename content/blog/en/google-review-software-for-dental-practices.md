## How did I pick these five?

I picked these five because each one solves a different version of the same problem: getting a happy patient to leave a Google review without the front desk having to ask. I judged them on four things.

- Does it send the request automatically after the visit, by text, email or WhatsApp?
- Does it help you reply, since unanswered reviews look careless?
- Does it stay inside Google's rules on incentives and selective solicitation?
- Can a practice in the UK or EU realistically buy it, and is the price published?

I left out any tool where I could not confirm what it costs or whether it is sold to a European practice. That is also why Podium is not here: I could not confirm UK availability for dental practices, and its [pricing page](https://www.podium.com/pricing) sends you to sales.

## 3. GatherUp

GatherUp is the self-serve option for a single practice. According to its [pricing page](https://www.gatherup.com/pricing), Reputation Management costs USD 99 per month for one location, or USD 60 per month per location for multiple locations, billed annually with month-to-month available. It includes review requests, QR codes, custom feedback links, up to 300 SMS and 3,000 email credits per location per month, and AI-crafted replies across 100 or more sites.

The page does not say how the Google request itself works beyond "feedback and review requests", so ask for a demo and check that every patient gets the Google link, not only the happy ones. Its custom feedback links are where a gating mistake could creep in.

- Best for: a single practice with someone willing to set up and monitor a tool.
- Price: USD 99 per month for one location, in US dollars.
- Why it stands out: published prices, AI replies and a generous message allowance.
- Worth knowing: someone on your team still has to feed it patient contacts and watch it, and the price is in USD, so your card statement will move with the exchange rate.

## 5. A free Google review link and QR code

Google lets you ask patients to leave a review through a link or a QR code, as described in its [business help page](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3474122). It costs nothing. You can put the QR code on appointment cards, the receipt, or a follow-up text. I built a [free Google review link generator](/tools/google-review-link-generator) so you can make yours in a minute and see it before you print it.

This is the right place to start, and for some practices it is enough. The weakness is not the link. It is that a person has to remember to send it, every time, to every patient.

- Best for: practices starting from zero reviews, or anyone who wants to test whether patients respond before paying for software.
- Price: free.
- Why it stands out: no contract, no software, and it follows Google's own method.
- Worth knowing: it is fully manual, so it works on the days someone remembers and stalls on busy ones. It also does nothing for replies.

## What are Google's rules on asking dental patients for reviews?

Google's rules say you may not offer incentives, and you may not selectively ask only happy customers. Its [contribution policy](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114) bars merchants from offering discounts, free goods or services in exchange for reviews, and from "selectively solicit positive reviews from customers". It also says not to pressure people to review while they are on the premises.

In practice that gives you three rules for any tool on this list:

- Send the same Google link to every patient, not only those who score you highly in a survey.
- Never tie a review to a discount, a whitening kit or a free check-up.
- Send the request after the visit, not at the desk with the patient standing there.

A private complaint channel is fine and good manners, as long as it sits beside the Google link and does not replace it for unhappy patients. If a tool offers "review filtering", walk away.

## A worked example: what 40 requests a week could look like

This is an illustration, not a client result. Say your practice sees about 40 patients a week and sends each a review request after the visit. If a modest share leave a review, you collect a handful every week without anyone chasing. A manual process, where the receptionist remembers on a good day, tends to reach only a fraction of those patients. The gap is the whole argument for automation: consistency matters more than clever wording.

The other half is replies. Even a few new reviews a week means a few replies a week, and that is where a practice manager's time quietly goes. Drafted replies that a person approves are the sensible middle.
