## Why do medspas get so many no-shows in the first place?

Because aesthetic bookings sit far in the future, involve nervous first-timers, and often carry no meaningful cancellation cost to the client. A consultation booked three weeks out is easy to forget. A first-time filler or laser client has time to talk themselves out of it. Someone who booked on a whim at 9pm may not even remember the exact date. None of these people are bad clients. They are just human, and a booking system that relies on them remembering is a system that leaks.

Medspas also feel each miss harder than most businesses. Your treatment slots are long, your practitioners are expensive, and the appointment that vanishes was often worth hundreds or thousands of euros in lifetime value, not a quick ten-minute visit. When a hairdresser loses a slot they lose 30 minutes. When you lose a laser package consultation, you lose the package, the follow-ups, and the referrals that client would have brought. The high value that makes aesthetics attractive is exactly what makes a no-show sting.

## How much are no-shows actually costing your medspa?

More than most owners think, because the real cost is the empty chair plus everything that chair would have led to. The direct loss is easy: your average treatment value times the number of missed appointments. But an unfilled high-ticket slot also carries the practitioner's paid time, the retail and package upsells that never happened, and the rebooking your front desk could have made if they had known the slot was free earlier.

## What is the difference between a reminder and a confirmation?

A reminder tells the client about the appointment; a confirmation asks them to do something about it. That distinction is where most of the no-show reduction lives. A one-way "you have an appointment tomorrow" text is better than nothing, but it does not surface the client who has already decided not to come. A confirmation asks the client to confirm, reschedule, or cancel, and it acts on the answer: if they cancel three days out, the slot reopens and your team can offer it to someone on the waitlist while there is still time to fill it.

The strongest setup layers both. A short message goes out 48 to 72 hours ahead so there is room to refill a freed slot, then a final nudge the day before. For high-value treatments and first-time clients, a live confirmation call adds a human touch and catches the people who ignore texts. The call also does something a message cannot: it answers a last-minute question ("do I stop retinol before this?") that might otherwise have quietly become the reason someone skipped.

## What should a medspa no-show reminder actually say?

Short, branded, specific, and easy to act on in one step. The clients who skip are busy or anxious, so a wall of text or a reply-with-nothing message does not help. Give them the clinic name, the treatment, the date and time, and a single clear action.

Use this as a starting checklist for your confirmation sequence:

- Send the first confirmation 48 to 72 hours ahead, worded so the client can confirm or reschedule in one reply or tap.
- Name the treatment and practitioner, not just "your appointment", so the booking feels real and specific.
- Include any prep instructions that clients commonly forget, since a missed prep step is a common reason people cancel late.
- Send a second, shorter reminder the day before as a final nudge.
- For new clients and high-ticket bookings, add a live confirmation call that can answer questions on the spot.
- Make cancelling frictionless on purpose: an early honest cancellation you can refill beats a silent no-show you cannot.
- Feed freed slots straight to a waitlist so an early cancellation becomes someone else's booked appointment.

That last point is where medspas leave the most money on the table. A no-show you learn about on the day is unrecoverable. A cancellation you learn about three days out is a slot you can sell twice.

## Should you charge deposits, or is that enough on its own?

Deposits reduce casual no-shows, but they work best alongside confirmations, not instead of them. Taking a card or a deposit at booking gives clients a reason to show up or cancel properly, and for expensive treatments it filters out tyre-kickers. The risk is friction: a deposit wall at the moment of booking can scare off a nervous first-timer who would have converted, and it does nothing for the client who genuinely forgot the date.

The pragmatic answer for most medspas is to use deposits selectively, on high-ticket or repeat-offender bookings, and to run automated confirmations across the board. The confirmation system catches the forgetful majority; the deposit handles the small minority who book with no intention of committing. Together they cover both failure modes without turning your booking flow into an obstacle course.
