Start with the single task that is costing you the most money right now. For most independent restaurants that is the phone ringing unanswered during service, then guests who book and do not turn up, then happy diners who never leave a review. Pick one, fix it, measure it for a month, and only then add the next.

## Why does the first step matter more than the tool?

The first step matters more than the tool because most restaurant owners do not fail at AI by choosing the wrong software. They fail by automating something that was never a real problem.

The common way this goes wrong is trying to do everything at once: AI menus, AI ordering and AI marketing in the same month, with nothing finished and a team that has stopped believing in it. A restaurant runs on thin margins and tired people, so every new thing has to earn its place quickly.

A good first automation has four traits:

- It touches money you can count (tables, covers, orders).
- It runs without your staff remembering to do anything.
- It connects to tools you already have.
- You can tell within a month whether it worked.

Everything on the checklist below passes those four tests. Fancy ideas usually do not.

## Step 1: Are you losing bookings to missed calls?

If your phone rings during the lunch or dinner rush and nobody can pick up, you are losing bookings to missed calls, and that is the first place I would look.

Think about who calls a restaurant. Someone planning a birthday for eight. Someone asking if you can take a walk-in table in twenty minutes. A regular who wants Friday. They rarely leave a voicemail. They ring the next place on the list.

Here is a quick way to check, and it costs nothing:

1. Look at your phone's call log for last Friday and Saturday between 12:00 and 15:00 and between 18:00 and 22:00.
2. Count the missed or unanswered calls.
3. Multiply by your average booking size, then by the share you think would have turned into a table (be honest, say one in three).

That gives you a rough weekly leak. When you read the log, separate three kinds of entry: numbers that rang once and never called back, numbers that rang several times in a row (someone really wanted a table), and numbers you recognise as regulars. The first group is your leak, the second is the most urgent, and the third tells you whether a simple text reply would be enough. If you want a cleaner number, [work out your own figure with our free missed call revenue calculator](/tools/missed-call-revenue-calculator).

What fixes it comes in two levels. The lighter one is a text sent automatically after a missed call, with a booking link; I wrote about it in [missed call text-back for restaurants](/blog/missed-call-text-back-for-restaurants). The fuller one is a voice assistant that answers the call itself, takes the booking and puts it in your system. Start with the lighter one if you are unsure, and move up if the numbers justify it.

## Step 2: How many guests book and then do not show up?

If more than a handful of covers a week vanish without a call, reservation reminders are your second step, because a no-show costs you a table you could have filled.

Reminders are the simplest automation there is. A WhatsApp or text message goes out the day before, asks the guest to confirm or cancel, and frees the table if they cancel. Nobody on your team has to remember to send it. If a guest cancels, you get a chance to re-offer the slot.

Check your own situation first:

- Do you take bookings by phone, WhatsApp, a booking site, or all three?
- Do you know how many no-shows you had last month? If not, start writing them down this week.
- Do you take deposits for larger groups? If not, a confirmation message is the gentlest alternative.

The maths changes by venue. A 12-seat bistro with one sitting loses a lot from two empty tables. A 120-cover place loses less per no-show but has more of them. The arithmetic is always the same: no-shows times average spend, and our [no-show cost calculator](/tools/no-show-cost-calculator) will do it for you.

## Step 3: Are your happy guests leaving reviews?

If your regulars love you but your Google profile has few recent reviews, an automatic review request is the third step, and it is the cheapest of the three to start.

The idea is plain. After a booking or a visit, the guest gets a short message with a direct link to your Google review page. You are not buying reviews or filtering who gets asked. You are removing the friction of finding the right page on a phone.

You can test this without paying anyone. Use our [free Google review link generator](/tools/google-review-link-generator) to make a direct link, put it on a card at the table or send it by hand to a few guests for two weeks. If reviews start to arrive, you know automating the message is worth it.

## What should wait until later?

Menu chatbots, full AI ordering, dynamic pricing and social media content generation can all wait. They are not bad ideas. They just need more setup, more of your attention, and a stable base underneath them.

My read is that a restaurant should earn its second layer of automation. When calls, reminders and reviews run quietly in the background, you will know what to ask for next, because you will have seen where the gaps are.

## A worked example: choosing between the three

This is an illustration, not a client story. Say you run a 40-seat neighbourhood restaurant.

| Signal in your numbers | What it points to | First step |
|---|---|---|
| 15 or more unanswered calls across a weekend | Phone coverage | Missed call replies or a voice assistant |
| 5 or more no-shows a week on booked tables | Reservation reminders | WhatsApp confirmation the day before |
| Fewer than 5 new reviews a month | Review requests | Review link after the visit |
| None of the above stand out | Your problem may be elsewhere | Book a free audit before spending anything |

The numbers are example thresholds, not industry benchmarks. Adjust them to your venue. The point is to let one number win, rather than choosing the option that sounds the most modern.

## What does a good first automation look like in practice?

A good first automation is narrow, visible and owned by someone. I can speak to one example from our own work: for A Difesa we built a Meta lead-gen bot. It is a different industry, but the pattern carries over to restaurants: one channel where messages arrive, one automation that handles the first reply, and a human who takes over when it matters.

For a restaurant, the equivalent is a phone line or WhatsApp inbox that never goes quiet, with your team stepping in for anything unusual. ## Your first-week checklist

Print this, tape it near the till, and work through it with your manager or chef.

1. Pull last weekend's call log and count unanswered calls during service.
2. Write down no-shows for the past two weeks, with party size.
3. Check how many Google reviews you got in the last 30 days.
4. Circle the biggest leak. Only one.
5. List the tools you already use: phone system, booking tool, WhatsApp, POS.
6. Decide who on your team owns the result, even if it is you.
7. Set a one-month review date and a number that counts as success.
