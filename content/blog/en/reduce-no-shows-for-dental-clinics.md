The most reliable way to reduce no-shows for dental clinics is to confirm every appointment twice and let the patient cancel or move it in one step, by voice if they ignore texts. An automated [voice assistant](/voice-ai) can do all three jobs: call to confirm, reschedule on the spot, and offer the freed slot to someone else. It does this without your front desk spending the afternoon on the phone.

I run Automis with my co-founder, and we build exactly this, so weigh my view accordingly. But the principle holds whoever you buy from: a no-show is rarely a patient who decided to skip. It is usually a patient who forgot, got stuck, or could not face the awkward call to cancel.

## How much do no-shows really cost a dental clinic?

A no-show costs you the fee for that treatment, plus the wages and rent that kept running while the chair sat empty. Your own figure is probably higher than your gut says, because most practices only count the invoice they did not raise.

Here is an example, clearly illustrative and not a benchmark. Say a practice books 100 appointments a week, loses 8 of them to no-shows, and the average appointment is worth £120. That is £960 a week. Over 48 working weeks it is £46,080 a year. If better reminders halved the problem, that is about £23,000 of chair time back in the diary.

## Why do dental patients miss appointments?

Patients miss dental appointments mostly because of forgetting, schedule clashes, anxiety and the friction of cancelling. The first two are solved by reminders. The last two need a different approach.

Forgetting is the big one. Appointments are booked months ahead for check-ups and hygiene visits, and nothing in the patient's week reminds them. Schedule clashes come next: work, childcare, a sick day. Those patients would happily move the slot if it took ten seconds.

Dental anxiety is real and a reminder alone will not fix it. Someone dreading a procedure may quietly let the time pass rather than ring and cancel. A friendly call that says "would you like to move it?" gives them an exit that is not shameful, and that is often enough to keep them in the relationship.

Then there is friction. If cancelling means waiting on hold, or the practice line goes to voicemail at lunch, the patient gives up. This is why missed calls and no-shows are linked, and why I would fix both together. ## How do automated voice reminders work?

Automated voice reminders ring the patient before the appointment, ask them to confirm, and write the answer straight into your diary. There are four moving parts.

First, the assistant reads upcoming appointments from your calendar or practice software. Second, at the times you set, it calls or messages the patient with the practice name, the day, the time and the clinician. Third, it listens. The patient says "yes, I'll be there", "I can't make it" or "can we do Thursday?", and the assistant understands plain speech rather than asking them to press 1. Fourth, it acts: marks the visit confirmed, moves it, or cancels it and flags the gap.

That last step is where voice beats a basic reminder. An SMS that says "reply C to cancel" tells you a slot is empty. A voice assistant that can also rebook means the patient ends the call with a new time, and you keep the revenue.

## Voice, SMS or WhatsApp: which reminder channel works best?

Use all three in a sequence, not one channel alone. My view is text first, call second.

| Channel | Best at | Weak at | Rough running cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| SMS | Cheap first nudge, works on any phone | Easy to ignore, no two-way booking | Pence per message |
| WhatsApp | Rich, familiar, replies in one tap | Needs patient opt-in, not everyone uses it | Low per conversation |
| Voice call | Gets a spoken yes or no, reaches older patients | Patients screen unknown numbers | Higher per contact, included in a flat fee with us |
| Front desk phone call | Personal touch for complex cases | Takes staff time, inconsistent | Wages |

A sensible flow looks like this. A text or WhatsApp message goes out a few days before with a confirm button. Anyone who has not answered gets a voice call the day before. Anyone who still has not answered is flagged to the front desk, who can phone the ones that matter, such as a long root canal appointment.

That sequence keeps the voice call for the patients who need it. It also stops the assistant from pestering people who confirmed instantly.

## What should a confirmation call say?

A good confirmation call is short, specific and gives a way out. Aim for under twenty seconds when the patient just says yes.

A script I would be happy with: "Hello, this is the assistant from [practice name]. I'm calling about your appointment with [clinician] on [day] at [time]. Can you still make it?" If yes: "Lovely, we'll see you then." If no: "No problem. I can offer Thursday at 10 or Friday at 2. Would either work?" And always: "If you'd rather speak to someone, I can put you through."

Three details matter more than they look. Say who is calling, so the patient does not hang up as a cold call. Name the clinician, because people confirm "Dr Rossi" more readily than "a dental appointment". And do not read out treatment details or clinical information on a call that might be answered by someone else. Keep the content to the booking.

## How does the freed-up slot get refilled?

A cancelled slot only helps you if someone else takes it, and speed is the whole game. Cancellations that arrive the day before are the hardest to fill by hand.

When the assistant records a cancellation, the slot can go to a waiting list, or to patients who asked for an earlier time. It can also go to the next person who rings the practice. If the same assistant answers your inbound calls, it already knows the gap exists, so a caller asking "any chance of this week?" gets offered it immediately. This is the practical reason to run reminders and call answering on one system rather than two.

## What is the data and consent position?

Reminders about an existing appointment are generally treated as service messages rather than marketing, but rules differ between the UK, the EU and individual countries. Keep calls to the booking itself, record the patient's contact preferences, and make opting out simple. Ask your data protection lead or adviser to approve the wording before it goes live. I am not a lawyer, and I would not want you to take a founder's word on this.

If a patient shares anything clinical during a call, the assistant should hand over to a person. Privacy design is part of the build, not an afterthought.

## How do you get started without overhauling everything?

Start with the part that costs you most and is easiest to measure. I would follow this order.

1. Run the [no-show cost calculator](/tools/no-show-cost-calculator) with your last three months of diary data.
2. Pick the appointment types that hurt most when missed: long procedures, new patient exams, hygienist slots.
3. Switch on a two-step reminder for those types only, a text then a call.
4. Check the diary after four weeks. Count confirmed, moved, cancelled and no-show.
5. Extend to every appointment type once the wording feels right.

Do not try to automate everything in week one. A small, measured start tells you whether the money is real before you commit to the rest.
