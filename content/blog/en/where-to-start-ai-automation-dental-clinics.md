Start with the phone. Recover the calls your clinic misses, then cut no-shows with automated confirmations, and only after that look at bigger projects such as lead generation or a full AI receptionist. Those two steps sit closest to revenue, need the least change from your team, and show results within weeks.

If you would rather have someone look at your clinic and tell you where the biggest leak is, our [Jumpstart Audit](/jumpstart-audit) does exactly that. If you prefer to work it out yourself, the rest of this article is the order I would follow.

## Why should a dental clinic start with missed calls?

A dental clinic should start with missed calls because every unanswered call is a patient who is actively trying to book, and the fix is small, cheap and easy to measure. Nothing else in the practice has such a short path between effort and a filled chair.

Think about when your phone goes unanswered. Lunch. The moment the receptionist is checking a patient in. A hygienist running late, so reception is handling the waiting room. After 6pm. Saturday mornings. The patient who gets voicemail rarely leaves a message. They ring the next practice on the list.

When we built the setup for Clinica Santa Maria, a dental clinic, missed calls were the first thing we looked at, followed by booking and no-shows. That order was deliberate. A missed call is a patient who has already decided to come in. Compare that with a cold advert, where you pay to convince someone first.

You can put a rough figure on it yourself. Count the calls your phone system logged as unanswered last month, guess how many were new patients, and multiply by your average first visit value. That is an estimate, not a promise, but it is usually enough to settle the argument about priorities. Our free [missed call revenue calculator](/tools/missed-call-revenue-calculator) does the arithmetic if you give it your own numbers.

### What does missed-call recovery actually look like?

There are two levels, and I would start with the simpler one.

The first is an automatic text or WhatsApp message sent within a minute of a missed call. It says the clinic is sorry to have missed them, asks what they need, and offers a link or a reply to book. We cover the details in our guide to [missed call text back for dental clinics](/blog/missed-call-text-back-for-dental-clinics).

The second is an assistant that answers the call itself, in your clinic's voice, takes the booking and writes it into the diary. That is a bigger project, and I would only do it after the first level proves there is demand you are losing.

## Why come second: no-shows?

No-shows come second because they are the other place where booked revenue quietly disappears, and the fix works with patients you already have. A reminder system costs less than winning a single new patient through advertising.

Every empty slot is lost chair time with fixed costs still running: the dentist, the nurse, the surgery, the lab work already prepared. The loss is bigger for long treatments such as crowns or implants than for a quick check.

What helps, in my experience of the clinics we talk to:

- A confirmation message a few days before, with a one-tap way to confirm, cancel or move the appointment.
- A second reminder the day before, only to patients who have not replied.
- A short waiting list that is offered freed slots automatically, so a cancellation does not become an empty chair.

The third point is the one most clinics skip, and it is where the money comes back. ## What order should the projects go in?

Do them in order of how close they sit to revenue and how little they disrupt the team. This is my ranking for a typical independent dental clinic.

| Step | Project | Effort for your team | What you are measuring |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Missed-call text back | Low | Missed calls turned into bookings |
| 2 | Appointment confirmations and reminders | Low | No-shows per month |
| 3 | Waiting-list refill for cancelled slots | Medium | Freed slots refilled |
| 4 | AI receptionist answering calls | Medium | Calls handled without staff |
| 5 | Lead follow-up from adverts and web forms | Medium | Enquiries that became bookings |
| 6 | Internal knowledge and document search | Higher | Staff time saved on lookups |

Steps one and two can run side by side. I would not start step four before step one has been live for a few weeks, because you need real data on what patients ask when they call. Step six is useful but rarely urgent for a clinic.

## What do you need in place before you start?

Before you start you need three things: a diary that something can write into, a clear idea of what a booking means in your clinic, and one person who owns the project. Everything else can be sorted along the way.

A short checklist you can run through this week:

1. Can your practice management system be connected to other tools, or does it only work as a closed box? Ask your supplier plainly.
2. Which treatments can be booked without a human, and which need a call back? New patient check ups usually can. Complex consultations may not.
3. What are the rules for booking length, dentist availability and emergency slots?
4. Who answers when the assistant hands over a conversation, and during which hours?
5. Which messages are you allowed to send? Check your consent wording and your privacy notice.
6. What are your starting numbers: missed calls, no-shows, front desk hours on confirmations?

The last item matters most. Without a baseline, in two months nobody will be able to say whether it worked.

## What mistakes should you avoid?

The common mistakes are starting too big, skipping the baseline, and ignoring the team. Each one is avoidable.

Starting too big means buying a full AI receptionist, a CRM and a marketing system at once. If something goes wrong you will not know which part caused it, and the team will feel it was done to them.

Skipping the baseline means you cannot prove the result, so the project gets quietly dropped at the next budget review.

Ignoring the team is the one I would watch most. Your receptionists are not being replaced here. They are being relieved of the repetitive calls and chasing so they can look after patients in front of them. Show them the first version before it goes live, and ask what the assistant would get wrong. They usually know.

Two more worth naming. Do not let a vendor put patients' clinical details into a general chat tool without a data processing agreement. And do not automate a process that is already messy: if your diary rules are inconsistent, fix them first, or the automation will repeat the inconsistency faster.

## What does a sensible first month look like?

A sensible first month has four short phases. This is a plan I would give a clinic owner, not a record of one specific client.

Week one: collect your baseline numbers and write down your booking rules. Agree who owns the project.

Week two: set up missed-call text back, tested on your own phone and your colleagues' phones before any patient sees it.

Week three: switch it on for real, and read every conversation it has. Fix wording and rules as you spot problems.

Week four: add confirmations and reminders, compare the numbers with your baseline, and decide whether step three or four is worth doing.

If at the end of the month the first two numbers have not moved, do not buy more. Find out why. Usually it is a booking rule, a missing hours setting, or a message that sounds stiff.

## So where do you actually begin?

Begin by measuring last month's missed calls and no-shows, then fix the bigger of the two. That takes an afternoon and costs nothing, and it tells you whether the first project is a text back or a reminder system.
